Acquisition to a joint GPS + Galileo position fix, in Python, from a 90-second capture on a hobby SDR. Plus a relativity experiment you can repeat.

Say (30 s): A GNSS receiver written from the spec, start to finish, that takes a raw IQ file from any SDR and produces a position — from GPS and Galileo together. It's a teaching receiver as much as a working one: every step is a readable function, and every number on these slides came out of it.
Why this room should care

Every GNSS textbook step, as code you can read — and it actually fixes.

  • GPS L1 C/A: parallel code-phase × Doppler FFT acquisition → carrier + code tracking → 50 bps nav decode → ephemeris → IS-GPS-200 Kepler solve with harmonic corrections, Sagnac, tropo/iono.
  • Galileo E1-B: CBOC acquisition, full I/NAV decoder — 1161 / 1161 pages CRC-clean across three satellites; Galileo System Time read off the air; the GPS–Galileo time offset decodes to +6.0 ns.
  • Joint fix: 6 GPS + 3 Galileo solved together with the inter-system offset as a fifth unknown.
  • Tables fetched at runtime; a --selftest synthesises a complete E1 signal and decodes it.

Lessons with teeth

Three bugs that looked like physics.

  • The Doppler-median bug: a median of Doppler samples instead of a fit meant every satellite with > 0.5 Hz/s rate decoded as noise — while acquisition looked perfectly healthy. Pi went 2/6 → 6/6. Bimodal results ⇒ stop blaming the signal.
  • Code milliseconds tick on the satellite's clock, not GPS time: assemble integer + fraction in SV time, then subtract the clock correction. A millisecond-ambiguity search had been papering over this since day one.
  • A gate that names its failure and then says SOLVED is worse than no gate: the receiver now refuses to report a fix it cannot justify.
  • The fun one: an eccentric satellite (e = 0.168) from the classic gravitational-redshift tests — the decoder read its elliptical ephemeris and its do-not-use flag, and the ±39 ns per-orbit relativistic clock term falls straight out.
4 / 6each lesson is a regression gate now
Say (55 s): Three bugs that wore the costume of a hardware limit. A median where a fit belonged silently killed every fast-moving satellite while the acquisition plots looked great. Millisecond assembly in the wrong time base. And a solver that reported success after describing its own failure. Each is now a test. The relativity one is just joy: the ±39 nanosecond eccentricity term is right there in the data.
Why it's fast enough to be useful

From 10 minutes to under a minute, with identical fixes.

  • Same fixes before and after to the metre — the speed campaign was gated on identity, not on "looks right".
  • Live capture on the desk: 9 m 55 s → 5 m 45 s. A contributed 90 s file: 6 m 30 s → 47 s.
  • --validate replays recorded captures with no radio — the regression suite a stranger can run.
  • SigMF sidecar guard: a capture whose sample count doesn't match wall-time × fs is VOID, never "a bit noisy".
  • Rejected along the way, with numbers: elevation weighting (no gain on these captures).
5 / 6python fix.py capture.sigmf --validate
Say (35 s): It went from ten minutes to under one, and the rule for every speed change was: the fix must be identical to the metre, or it doesn't ship. Captures carry a SigMF sidecar, and a file whose sample count disagrees with its wall clock is declared void rather than excused.
